Seismic Wave Propagation in Earth's Interior

Seismic wave propagation through Earth's crust and mantle follows the elastic wave equation, which describes how mechanical disturbances travel through solid and fluid media. Understanding these waves is fundamental to seismology, earthquake hazard assessment, and subsurface imaging for resource exploration.

P-waves (primary or compressional waves) are the fastest seismic waves, traveling through both solids and liquids. They compress and expand the material in the direction of wave travel, similar to sound waves in air. In typical crustal rocks, P-wave velocities range from 5 to 8 km/s, depending on composition and depth.

S-waves (secondary or shear waves) travel slower than P-waves and can only propagate through solids. They move material perpendicular to the direction of wave travel. The absence of S-waves in Earth's outer core provided the first evidence that this region is liquid.

Surface waves, including Rayleigh and Love waves, travel along Earth's surface and cause the most damage during earthquakes. Rayleigh waves produce a retrograde elliptical motion, while Love waves cause horizontal shearing. Their amplitudes decrease with depth, making them particularly sensitive to near-surface structure.

When seismic waves encounter boundaries between rock layers with different elastic properties, they undergo reflection, refraction, and mode conversion. Snell's law governs the angles of transmitted and reflected waves. At critical angles, head waves travel along interfaces, providing information about deep layer velocities.

Seismic attenuation, described by the quality factor Q, causes amplitude decay beyond geometric spreading. Intrinsic attenuation converts wave energy to heat through anelastic processes, while scattering attenuation redistributes energy due to small-scale heterogeneities. Both effects are frequency-dependent, with higher frequencies attenuating more rapidly.

Full waveform inversion (FWI) exploits the complete wavefield, including amplitudes and phases, to construct high-resolution subsurface models. This technique requires accurate forward modeling using numerical methods such as finite-difference or spectral-element methods, and iterative optimization to minimize the misfit between observed and synthetic seismograms.
